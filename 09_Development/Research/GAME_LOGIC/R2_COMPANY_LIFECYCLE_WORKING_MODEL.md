# R2 — Company Life-Cycle Working Model

Status: **RESEARCH WORKING PAPER — NON-CANONICAL**  
Parent: #423  
Depends on: #424 / provisional R1 owner direction

## Purpose

Research the complete life of a company in DROPi Tycoon from an incumbent starter employer through player-founded local firms, mature regional/national companies, industrial ownership, multinational logistics and eventual failure, acquisition or legacy.

This paper does not change canon.

---

# 1. Companies pre-exist the player

A fresh World Instance cannot begin with an empty economy waiting for players to create every employer.

The baseline world must contain a governed mix of:

- incumbent logistics/delivery companies;
- shops and merchants;
- farms;
- factories/industrial producers;
- utility/infrastructure operators;
- civic/public service abstractions;
- other baseline organizations required for starter employment and essential supply.

These may be simulation-owned/NPC organizations at world launch and can later interact with, employ, contract with, compete with, be invested in or in some cases be acquired by human players under future rules.

The world therefore remains economically playable even at low human population.

---

# 2. Starter employer role

The starter incumbent logistics employer exists to convert real economic demand into paid entry work.

It must not exist merely as an infinite mission dispenser.

Conceptual flow:

```text
people / shops / organizations need transport
-> demand becomes a commercial service need
-> incumbent company wins/owns/routes that service
-> company creates shifts/routes
-> company employs human/NPC workers
-> workers perform service
-> company receives customer/contract revenue
-> company pays wages and operating expenses
```

The owner used Amazon as a concrete reference for this employer. Final brand treatment remains unresolved research.

---

# 3. Company financial boundary

A company has its own money and cannot freely use the founder's Personal Money after formation.

Core flows:

## Money enters a company through governed sources such as
- customer/service revenue;
- contract settlement;
- sale of goods/products;
- infrastructure/service fees;
- owner/investor capital contribution;
- treasury-share issuance settlement;
- loans/financing only after debt research;
- public concession/subsidy only if R6 later authorizes it.

## Money leaves through
- wages/payroll;
- fuel/electricity/energy;
- maintenance/repairs;
- rent/land/property costs;
- utilities;
- equipment/vehicles;
- inventory/raw materials;
- training;
- permits/authorization fees where modeled;
- insurance/tax/financing only when separately authorized;
- infrastructure construction;
- dividends from real distributable profit;
- waste treatment/disposal where applicable.

A company can be profitable on paper but fail through cash-flow shortage.

---

# 4. Player transition from employee to entrepreneur

Owning a company is not a level reward.

A player-founded company may require a stack including:

- sufficient Personal Money or legitimate financing;
- entrepreneurship/business qualification;
- local authorization/company-capacity access;
- minimum service capability;
- suitable registration/physical base;
- required equipment or lease arrangements;
- initial operating cash;
- any mandatory specialist capacity for the chosen activity.

Formation settlement should conceptually be:

```text
Personal Money / authorized finance
-> explicit capital contribution / startup settlement
-> new Company Money
-> separate company ledger begins
```

Personal and Company Money remain separate thereafter.

---

# 5. Company capability stack

A company may perform an activity only when the organization owns/controls the required combination of:

- qualified workforce;
- operational management/authority;
- vehicles/equipment;
- fuel/energy;
- maintenance capacity;
- facilities/departments;
- inventory/raw materials;
- contracts/customers;
- infrastructure access;
- permits/authorization abstractions;
- working capital;
- technology/research;
- geographic rights/access;
- service reputation where required.

Money alone cannot substitute for the entire stack.

# 7. Local delivery-company competition

Existing owner direction should be preserved for research:

- dense urban/locality markets should not contain unlimited equivalent delivery companies;
- target design: up to roughly **5 meaningful delivery competitors in an urban locality**;
- rural localities should normally support **1–2 meaningful delivery competitors** because demand and density are lower.

These are current owner design constraints, but final market-capacity mechanics require R4/R5 simulation.

The cap must not permanently exclude humans. Entry paths may include:

- acquisition of an existing company;
- expansion into another locality;
- specialization into an underserved service;
- concession/authorization turnover;
- merger/restructuring;
- new settlement growth creating new capacity.

---

# 8. Customers and service demand

A company should not receive orders randomly.

Every service opportunity should trace to a cause such as:

- household consumption;
- merchant restocking;
- business procurement;
- industrial inputs;
- industrial outputs;
- agriculture;
- construction;
- waste removal;
- utility service;
- personal relocation/travel;
- public/institutional need;
- event-induced scarcity or demand.

This allows customer demand to shrink if population/business activity declines and grow if the city develops.

---

# 9. Occasional work -> commercial contract

The owner's industrial relationship model becomes a core company progression path.

Example:

```text
steelworks has occasional transport need
-> delivery/logistics company accepts one-off jobs
-> performance history accumulates
-> steelworks offers recurring service agreement / tender
-> company dedicates capacity
-> contract becomes important revenue stream
-> relationship can expand into warehousing / multimodal transport
-> mature company may invest in / acquire the productive asset if later rules permit
```

The same pattern can apply to:

- farms;
- chemical plants;
- paper mills;
- food factories;
- mines;
- ports;
- warehouses;
- retailers;
- utilities.

---

# 10. Productive-asset acquisition

Late-game company ownership may extend beyond logistics.

A company may eventually acquire/operate:

- farms;
- factories;
- steelworks;
- chemical plants;
- paper mills;
- warehouses/distribution centers;
- power/energy assets where allowed;
- rail terminals and later railway operations;
- ports/airport cargo infrastructure;
- other productive assets.

Acquisition must not be a shop button unlocked by level.

Potential prerequisites:

- sufficient company capital/valuation;
- sellers/auction/privatization/acquisition opportunity;
- governance approval;
- relevant specialists;
- operating licenses/concessions;
- infrastructure connectivity;
- input supply chain;
- product demand;
- environmental/waste handling where applicable;
- working capital for continued operation.

The exact privatization, concession and public/private ownership model belongs partly to R6.

---

# 11. Vertical integration

A mature company may choose to own more of its supply chain.

Example:

```text
logistics company
-> contracts with farm
-> buys/leases warehouse
-> acquires food processor
-> distributes finished products
-> sells into high-price city markets
```

Potential benefit:
- margin capture;
- supply security;
- routing control;
- product differentiation.

Potential cost/risk:
- capital lock-up;
- payroll;
- energy;
- maintenance;
- input shortages;
- inventory risk;
- price risk;
- specialist shortage;
- regulation/authorization;
- waste/by-product obligations.

Vertical integration is therefore strategy, not an automatic upgrade.

---

# 12. Workforce

Companies can employ both human and NPC/simulated workers under one economic truth.

A worker must be paid according to the applicable employment relationship and must contribute actual capability/output.

Human players can occupy meaningful positions without owning the company.

Company workforce may later include:

- couriers;
- drivers;
- dispatchers;
- warehouse operators;
- mechanics;
- managers;
- accountants/finance specialists;
- engineers;
- rail/air/maritime specialists;
- drone specialists;
- production workers;
- researchers;
- other domain specialists.

NPC substitution protects continuity but should not make human labor meaningless. R3 must research relative productivity, scarcity, wages and human/NPC substitution rules.

---

# 13. Company energy and resource metabolism

Like the hero and city, a company consumes resources.

Depending on activity, a company may consume:

- electricity;
- fuel;
- water;
- raw materials;
- packaging;
- spare parts;
- software/services;
- land/building capacity;
- labor time;
- financial capital.

And may produce:

- logistics services;
- products;
- inventory;
- waste;
- wastewater;
- recyclable material;
- emissions/pollution abstractions;
- by-products.

This is mandatory for productive industries and relevant for logistics facilities.

---

# 14. Company distress ladder

Companies need real failure states.

```text
HEALTHY
-> margin pressure
-> cash-flow stress
-> missed investment/maintenance
-> service degradation
-> customer/contract loss
-> payroll/creditor stress
-> restructuring
-> insolvency
-> sale / acquisition / bankruptcy / controlled closure
```

Failure must be causal rather than an arbitrary game-over timer.

Potential recovery paths:

- cut unprofitable routes;
- sell assets;
- renegotiate contracts;
- raise legitimate capital;
- replace management;
- merge;
- sell company/control;
- specialize;
- exit a market;
- enter formal restructuring.

---

# 15. Company bankruptcy must not erase the world

If a company fails:

- employees survive as people and can seek work;
- customers seek alternative suppliers;
- owned assets enter governed sale/liquidation/restructuring states;
- contracts terminate/transfer under rules;
- infrastructure with public significance cannot simply vanish;
- historical company identity can remain in the world record;
- the founder remains historically the founder;
- players can later form or join other organizations.

This creates economic consequences without deleting human identity.

---

# 16. Founder inactivity

A company must not freeze because its founder stopped playing.

Possible continuity mechanisms to research:

- delegated executive authority;
- governance succession;
- professional management;
- NPC operational management at a cost;
- board/shareholder action;
- eventual restructuring if no authority remains.

Founder history is permanent; active executive authority is not.

---

# 17. Mergers and acquisitions

M&A is a natural late-game consequence of a persistent economy and should be researched rather than bolted on later.

Possible transactions:

- asset purchase;
- company acquisition;
- merger;
- majority-control change;
- distressed acquisition;
- privatization/acquisition of eligible simulation-owned assets;
- concession transfer.

Every transaction requires authoritative ownership and money settlement and anti-collusion/anti-griefing rules.

---

# 18. Geographic expansion

Company expansion is not a map unlock alone.

To enter a new city/region/country a company may require:

- demand/business case;
- authorization;
- physical network connection;
- local depot/warehouse/partner;
- vehicles and specialist staff;
- operating capital;
- transport infrastructure;
- cross-border/customs abstractions where later modeled;
- local competition capacity.

A company may therefore be highly profitable locally but unable to expand internationally until organizational capability catches up.

---

# 19. Company late game

Money alone cannot remain the late-game objective.

Company goals may include:

- service reliability at enormous scale;
- strategic infrastructure operation;
- global supply-chain resilience;
- product/industry leadership;
- city/country development influence;
- specialist workforce excellence;
- research/technology;
- governance and investor trust;
- surviving macroeconomic shocks;
- restoring declining regions;
- legacy/history/prestige;
- competition with other mature human companies.

## Research rule

**A company is not a reward multiplier. It is a separate living economic organism with cash flow, workforce, assets, customers, resource consumption, productive output, waste, risk, ownership and history. It grows only when it can finance and operate the capability stack required for the next activity, and it can decline or fail when costs, demand, management or supply no longer sustain it.**
